Operating Spec

Approval-in-the-Loop

How Afferentic agents hand work back to the human when the stakes justify it. A safety net that exists while trust is being earned, and fades away as it is. Written for internal reference and customer-onboarding conversations. Prepared April 2026.

The Spec

1. Why this matters

Automation only earns adoption if the operator can feel the seatbelt on day one. A CFO who has signed off every price change for fifteen years will not hand that gesture to a black box because the demo was impressive. They will hand it over if, for the first few weeks, the agent lets them keep the gesture — with less friction.

Approval-in-the-loop is the mechanism for that handover. It is not a feature we sell. It is the posture the system adopts while the relationship is new.

The design constraint. The operator should not have to learn a new tool to approve something. An email with a visible button is where they already live. They tap, the work proceeds, Afferentic keeps the trail. Adoption is won in the two seconds between reading the email and tapping the button.

2. The canonical story — Ben at DHP

Ben runs pricing at a distribution company. His afferent reads the market feed, checks the stock positions, and prepares a set of price edits every morning at 07:30. The first month he wants to approve each batch himself — not because he expects the agent to be wrong, but because he hasn’t yet seen it be right often enough to trust it without looking.

At 07:31 his phone buzzes. The email opens with the numbers he cares about: how many edits, the average change, the largest single change. Underneath, a table of the proposed prices. Underneath that, three buttons: Approve, Edit, Decline.

He taps Approve. The agent applies the edits. A confirmation lands back in the same thread five seconds later. Ben drinks his coffee.

Three weeks later, Ben has seen forty consecutive approvals where the agent was right. He doesn’t want to approve the small ones any more. He moves the afferent down a rung on the confidence ladder — now it auto-applies edits below a threshold, and only emails him when a change exceeds it. A month after that, it handles the routine edits silently, and emails him only when the proposal sits outside the baseline the system has learned for him. Eventually, the email stops arriving most days, and that is the correct outcome.

What Ben bought. Not automation. A system that let him keep doing his job, with less friction, while the agent earned the right to take parts of it off his plate.

3. The four outcomes

When an approval lands in front of the operator, there are four things they can do with it. Three are decisions about the proposal itself; the fourth is a signal about the world the proposal was reasoning over. They are independent: an operator can Approve and Flag, because “this is right, but I’m nervous about the data” is a coherent and important thing to express.

Four doors out of the review; one feedback record; three ways that feedback makes the system smarter.

APPROVE

The proposal is right. Apply it. The straight-through path — most approvals at steady state.

EDIT

The shape is right, the detail isn’t. Remove rows, modify values, add something the afferent missed, then apply the corrected set. Typed mutations, validated by schema.

DECLINE

Not right this time. With a typed reason: not this way, not yet, needs more, needs less, won’t fix. Each reason tells the afferent what to do differently.

FLAG

Orthogonal to the other three. Says “the real issue is upstream — in the data, the mapping, or the afferent’s logic”. Attaches to any decision. Signal is routed to whoever can fix it.

Why the four-outcome model matters commercially. A customer who only has a binary Approve/Decline has to choose between being a rubber stamp or a blocker. The richer vocabulary is what turns the operator from a bouncer into a coach — each correction is a contribution, not a rejection. That’s the difference between an approval system that gets circumvented and one people actually want to use.

4. Walkthrough — London’s Dry Gin

The best way to feel how the four outcomes fit together is to follow a real case. Ben receives his morning stock-pricing proposal. It has 50 rows. Row 47 reads:

› Update Tanqueray Gold to £24.50 (from £23.00)

Ben blinks. Tanqueray Gold isn’t a DHP SKU — the product that takes a morning price adjustment on that feed row is London’s Dry Gin, at a different price point. He’s seen this happen before when someone hand-keyed a row into the upstream spreadsheet and got the SKU label wrong.

Here is what he does, all from a single email, in under a minute:

Step What Ben does What the system records
Reads the batch Eye down the 50 rows; everything else looks sane; row 47 doesn’t. —
Hits Edit Removes the Tanqueray Gold row. Adds a London’s Dry Gin row with the correct price. Leaves the other 49 untouched. edit.remove[row 47]
edit.add[london's-dry-gin]
Hits Flag on the removed row Picks upstream data. Types: “SKU wrong in morning feed row 47 — should be London’s Dry Gin.” flags: [{type: upstream_data, note: …}]
Hits Approve Approves the corrected batch of 50 rows (49 original + 1 replacement). status: approved; edit_diff logged
Apply fires The 50 approved rows go live. No Tanqueray Gold update ever leaves the building. Apply result recorded per action.
Flag dispatches Email lands with pricing-data@dhp.co.uk: “Operator corrected row 47 of morning feed — please verify the upstream SKU.” Signal tracked; ticket open until closed.
Next morning Afferent reads its own feedback before proposing. Sees: “Tanqueray Gold flagged upstream this week.” Emits proposal with a low-confidence SKU warning on any Tanqueray row. Afferent behaviour shifts without code change.
A week later Pattern detector sees five flags on the same SKU in seven days. Raises a meta-improvement. New approval in Ben’s queue: “Improvement: add SKU validation step to dhp-stock-pricing before proposing.” with the five flag records as evidence.
What just happened, commercially. Ben kept the correct pricing moving (the 49 good rows + 1 corrected). He told the data team exactly where their spreadsheet broke. He made the afferent more cautious on that SKU for next time. And he contributed to a durable fix that will land as its own reviewable improvement. Four actions, one minute, nothing falls on the floor. That story is what the portal and the email have to earn the right to deliver.

The hard part isn’t the model. It’s the portal and the email making four distinct decisions feel like “just replying to an email”. The operator should never have to learn the taxonomy — the buttons, tooltips, and defaults have to carry it. If Ben ever has to think “wait, is this a decline or a flag?”, the UX failed.

5. The flow, end to end

1. PREPARE

Afferent runs. Produces a typed artifact — goal, constraints, the specific actions it wants to take, why.

→

2. STASH & NOTIFY

Artifact parked server-side, keyed by a signed token. Email sent, badge lit in Tamar, entry in the approvals queue.

→

3. DECIDE

Operator hits Approve, Edit, or Decline from email or Tamar. No login required for Approve — the signature is the auth.

→

4. APPLY

On Approve: the apply step re-checks preconditions (the world may have moved) then executes the actions exactly as prepared.

→

5. CONFIRM

Result posted back — same email thread, Tamar, audit log. Outcome feeds the confidence ladder.

Each step is short. The agent does not hold a session open waiting for the operator to reply. The preparation step ends, the apply step fires when Approve is hit, and only then. This is what keeps the cost sane.

6. The proposal artifact

The agent does not hand the operator a paragraph of prose. It hands them a typed document with a known shape. The shape makes approvals skimmable, edits safe, and apply mechanical.

{
  "proposal_id":   "stock-price-2026-04-17-0730",
  "afferent":      "dhp-stock-pricing",
  "tenant":        "dhp",
  "prepared_at":   "2026-04-17T07:30:14Z",
  "expires_at":    "2026-04-17T23:30:00Z",
  "confidence":    "manual_every_time",

  "goal":          "Align stock prices with morning market feed",
  "constraints":   ["no single edit > 5%", "no negative margin"],
  "rationale":     "Market moved 1.2% overnight; recommended rebalance per policy-v3.",

  "actions": [
    { "type": "price.update", "sku": "AX-400", "from": 12.40, "to": 12.55, "reason": "market+1.2%" },
    { "type": "price.update", "sku": "AX-410", "from": 18.20, "to": 18.35, "reason": "market+1.2%" }
  ],

  "validation": [
    { "check": "all-skus-exist",       "required": true },
    { "check": "no-price-below-cost",  "required": true }
  ],

  "audit": {
    "sources":     ["feed://bloomberg/eod/2026-04-17", "db://dhp/stock/snapshot/..."],
    "policy_ref":  "pricing-policy-v3",
    "runbook":     "/runbooks/dhp/stock-pricing.md"
  }
}
Why structure matters. Most approvals need zero LLM at the apply step — the actions are typed and the runner just executes them. Edits are typed mutations with schema validation. Audit is free because every proposal is already a record. And when a conversation is genuinely needed, the artifact is the context you hand to the next run.

7. One-click from email

The approval email is not a summons to log into a portal. It is the decision itself, rendered inline.

What the operator sees.

  • — A headline number (how many actions, the single biggest one).
  • — A table of the proposed actions, clearly typeset, truncated sensibly if long.
  • — Three buttons underneath: Approve, Edit, Decline.
  • — A link into Tamar with the same content, if they prefer to review it there.
  • — The rationale, under a collapsible “Why”.

What each button does.

  • Approve — signed URL to /approvals/:token/approve. No login. The endpoint re-checks the signature, the TTL, and the preconditions, then fires the apply run. Idempotent.
  • Decline — same shape, different endpoint. Records a reason if provided.
  • Edit — opens Tamar to the proposal, typed form per action. Operator flips rows off, adjusts numbers, saves. The edit is validated by the schema before Approve is even offered.
Security posture. The signed link is enough for Approve on low-stakes actions. Anything above a policy-defined threshold requires the click to be followed by a Tamar login — the email merely starts the journey; the heavy-footed actions land in a session. Tokens are single-use, TTL-bound, and every click is audited whether or not it led to apply.

Behaviour

8. The confidence ladder

Every afferent has a confidence_policy that the operator sets. It starts strict and relaxes as the afferent earns it.

PolicyBehaviourWhen to use
manual_every_timeEvery run emails the operator. Nothing applies without an Approve.First weeks. High-stakes work (pricing, billing, external comms).
manual_above_thresholdSmall changes auto-apply. Changes above a typed threshold (amount, count, blast radius) email the operator.Once the afferent has a track record on the routine cases.
manual_on_anomalyThe system learns a baseline (via Gemma4). Proposals within the baseline auto-apply. Outliers are surfaced for approval.Stable afferents where the shape of normal work is well understood.
autoFully autonomous. Audit trail still kept. Operator can subscribe to a digest rather than individual decisions.Afferents that have proven themselves for months.
The ladder is operator-controlled. Afferentic does not silently promote an afferent up the ladder. The operator sees a prompt — “this afferent has been right 40 times in a row, ready to let it handle small changes on its own?” — and makes the move deliberately. Moving down the ladder is also one click, and we don’t need a reason.

9. Edit without a conversation

Edit is where most systems get dragged into chat interfaces that nobody asked for. Ours does not.

Because the proposal is typed, editing it is schema-level. The Tamar form for a proposal renders each action as a row with clear controls — checkbox to include, number inputs with min/max guards, inline validation. The operator turns off row 3, nudges the price on row 7, saves. The schema + policy check run immediately. If the edit is valid, Approve becomes live. If it isn’t, the validator tells them exactly what’s wrong in the same language the agent uses.

What this buys.

  • — No agent is invoked for most edits. Zero cost, zero latency.
  • — The edits the operator makes are themselves signal for the learning loop — “this operator consistently caps price changes at 3%” becomes a constraint proposal at the next monthly review.
  • — The audit trail distinguishes agent decisions from operator overrides automatically.

10. When conversation is needed

Sometimes the operator wants to ask a question, not edit a row. “Why did you pick SKU AX-400 to move 1.2% when the feed actually moved 0.9%?”

Two answers, in order of cost:

  1. Show the rationale. The proposal already carries the reasoning trace. Most questions are answered by displaying what the agent already said it was doing. No inference.
  2. Reconsider. If the question is really “reconsider the whole batch given this new constraint”, we fire a fresh Opus run with the original proposal plus the operator’s feedback as input. Short because the proposal is structured. Returns a new proposal that goes through the same Approve/Edit/Decline cycle.
The thing we specifically don’t do. We do not hold a long-running conversational session over an approval. It adds cost, complicates the architecture, and — for the actual work operators do — turns out to be the wrong shape almost always. Questions get answered from the trace. Reconsiderations get a fresh, cheap run. Everything else was probably an edit.

Rigour

11. Risks & mitigations

RiskMitigation
Leaked signed link Short TTL (24h default, shorter for higher-stakes). Single-use tokens. Device-bound on first click. High-stakes approvals require login after click, not just the click.
Stale state — the world moved The apply step re-checks preconditions (“are these still the current prices?”) before executing. If the preconditions fail, it aborts and re-proposes rather than forcing the stale action through.
Email never arrives DKIM/SPF tight from day one. Tamar shows the same pending approval with a visible badge on the dashboard. A digest email at end of day lists anything pending so nothing can silently rot in a spam folder.
Confidence ladder promotes too fast The operator promotes, not the system. We surface the option; they make the call. Demotion is always one click and requires no justification.
Operator approves without reading Email lead with the headline number (biggest single change, total blast radius). If the headline is alarming, the eye catches it. For high-stakes afferents, require a confirmation step (“I’ve read this and I approve”) — not just a one-click.
Cost creep from reconsiderations Reconsiderations are budgeted per afferent. Hit the budget and the system says so out loud rather than silently truncating.

12. Alternatives we rejected

Conversational session held open during approval

Uses the Claude SDK with a long-lived session. Feels interactive but holds compute and tokens while the operator decides. For approvals that sit for hours or days, the cost and complexity are wrong. Rejected.

Gemma4 as a conversational middleman

Keep the proposer as Opus, use the local Gemma4 for back-and-forth during review. Cheap and cute, but introduces two models with diverging worldviews, and Gemma4 is weaker on re-planning — which is exactly where conversation matters. Rejected as the default. Gemma4 stays in the loop, but narrower: classifying edits as trivial-vs-constraint-changing, and learning per-operator baselines.

Email-only approvals, no Tamar surface

Simpler to build, but fragile: no audit surface for the operator, no way to handle Edit, and anything missed in email is invisible. Email stays the one-click entry point; Tamar is the source of truth.

Tamar-only approvals, no email

Assumes the operator lives in Tamar. Most operators don’t — they live in email. Forcing a login per approval is friction that kills adoption on day one. Rejected.

13. What to build first

A concrete cut, in the order that de-risks the design:

  1. Approval artifact schema — TypeScript types + JSON schema. Used by the proposer to write, by Tamar to render, by the apply runner to execute.
  2. Approvals API on Tamar — POST /api/approvals (park), GET /api/approvals/:id, POST /api/approvals/:id/approve|decline|edit, with signed-link auth for approve and token-based auth for edit.
  3. Approvals tab in Tamar — queue view, proposal detail with typed form, audit history. Same component library as the rest of Tamar.
  4. Email template — clean, brand-consistent, buttons with signed URLs. Built once, used by every afferent that needs approvals.
  5. Automation-hook helper — request_approval "title" artifact.json from any afferent script. Returns when a decision lands (or times out cleanly).
  6. First afferent migration — pick a safe one (the DHP stock-pricing afferent if that client engagement is live, otherwise an internal one) and wire it through end to end. Prove the shape before rolling out.
  7. Confidence policy UI — per-afferent dropdown, with the “promote?” prompt logic. Ship last, once the cycle is working.
The point of this order. Every step produces something the operator can touch. The schema alone is of no use; the API plus a ugly Tamar page lets a real afferent run end-to-end. Build what earns the next decision, not what completes the feature list.
